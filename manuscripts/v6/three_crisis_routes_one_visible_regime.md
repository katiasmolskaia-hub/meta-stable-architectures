# Three Crisis Routes, One Visible Regime

## Why this problem matters

A network may look equally bad at the visible level:

- high turbulence,
- high error,
- weak gatherability,
- poor recovery.

But this does not mean that the crisis emerged in the same way.

The same visible crisis state may be reached through different routes.
If the route is different, then:

- the diagnosis is incomplete if it only describes the surface state,
- the right help may differ,
- and the correct re-entry path may differ as well.

This is one of the central opening problems of Volume VI.

## Route 1: Drift Route

### Surface pattern

The field gradually loses coherence:

- attention blurs,
- weak links become too weak,
- the overall form does not collapse immediately,
- but slowly disperses.

### Deeper cause

The cause is not a sharp blow.
It is a gradual erosion of maintained relation.

Weak bonds become too weak because:

- attunement decays,
- weak ties stop being sufficiently reinforced,
- local mismatch accumulates slowly,
- the field does not actively re-bind,
- and the system begins to economize in the wrong place by ceasing to sustain fine coordination.

In short:

- the bonds do not abruptly break;
- they stop being renewed.

### What kind of help fits

This route calls for:

- re-gathering,
- renewed shared rhythm,
- local support,
- and restoration of weak but meaningful ties.

## Route 2: Shock Route

### Surface pattern

The field is hit abruptly:

- suppression rises fast,
- coherence drops sharply,
- residual load remains high,
- and recovery tails become heavy.

### Deeper cause

The cause is not slow relational erosion.
It is a discontinuity between current field capacity and incoming load.

The system is struck faster than it can redistribute, absorb, or interpret.

This means:

- the field is overwhelmed before it can adapt,
- local regulation saturates,
- the instructor may arrive too late unless anticipation exists,
- and residual burden remains after the visible shock window closes.

In short:

- the crisis comes from overload before coordinated compensation can begin.

### What kind of help fits

This route calls for:

- protective pause,
- rapid load buffering,
- temporary slowing,
- and only later, guided re-entry into shared coordination.

## Route 3: Contagion Route

### Surface pattern

The crisis spreads through the network:

- local instability emerges in one region,
- neighboring agents begin to inherit it,
- turbulence rises in waves,
- and the field loses structure through propagation.

### Deeper cause

The cause is not just stress level.
It is relational transmissibility of bad local structure.

Weak links become harmful not because they vanish, but because they pass destabilization onward.

This means:

- local mismatch becomes socially transmissible,
- unstable nodes continue to influence neighbors,
- the field lacks enough stabilizing counter-links,
- and no positive contagion is yet strong enough to offset the bad spread.

In short:

- the crisis comes from the wrong thing spreading efficiently.

### What kind of help fits

This route calls for:

- suppression of bad spread,
- selective weakening of harmful links,
- support of stabilizing local structure,
- and eventually a form of stabilizing contagion rather than destructive contagion.

## Core diagnostic insight

The same visible bad regime does not imply:

- the same origin,
- the same field memory,
- or the same re-entry logic.

Therefore:

- state diagnosis is not enough;
- route diagnosis is also needed.

## Re-entry consequence

Different crisis routes imply different ways back:

- drift needs re-gathering,
- shock needs protective buffering and delayed re-entry,
- contagion needs re-bonding and stabilizing spread.

So re-entry into the field should not mean:

- returning the system to one abstract normal state.

It should mean:

- restoring gatherability in a manner that fits the route by which the crisis was entered.
